# Literature review

## Review questions and selection
This targeted scoping review asks which EDMS adoption issues matter, how workflow correctness should be represented, and how access control affects document processing. Searches on 19 October 2026, supplemented on 23 October 2026, used combinations of electronic document management system, electronic records, workflow, business process management, digital transformation, authorization, access control and Kazakhstan. Publisher pages, author-hosted papers and official technical documentation were preferred.

The scientific core contains ten publications. Eight date from 2022–2025 and fall within the preceding five years. Two foundational papers from 1996 and 1998 explain RBAC and explicit workflow reasoning. Technical documentation supports implementation decisions but does not count toward the scientific publications. The complete register contains 30 meaningfully used sources.

Selection required identifiable authors, a scholarly venue and a direct connection to the review questions. Broad promotional articles and unverifiable papers were excluded. This is not a claimed exhaustive database search or a PRISMA systematic review. Some publisher pages expose only abstracts and selected sections. The table states the inspected evidence, and no detailed findings beyond that evidence are asserted. Before final diploma submission, deepen the review with full texts and record any changed conclusions.

## Critical synthesis
EDMS reviews [S01, S02] and the DMS adoption survey [S26] support treating adoption, ease of use and staff capability as part of the problem. The survey reports associations rather than causal effects. Ten reported Astana conversations provide qualitative local examples of version, status and approval-history problems ([interview analysis](02_relevance.md)); they do not establish how often these problems occur or the effect size of a new system. Accordingly, we retain usability and onboarding requirements and will test them in a defined pilot workflow.

The Kazakhstan archive study [S27] gives local documentary context, but addresses state archives and is based on theoretical investigation. The ten interview notes provide more direct workflow examples, yet they span different sectors and do not establish one organisation's policy. Neither source justifies adding legally binding signatures or government integration to this project's first-stage scope.

BPM work [S03, S05, S06] supports analysing a process before automating it. Bibliometric activity is not evidence that our system improves performance. The project therefore selects concrete tasks and a baseline rather than claiming innovation because it uses a web interface. The custom-system hypothesis is a small, visible approval journey with ownership, a frozen review version and a recorded next action.

The access-control review and RBAC foundation [S04, S07] motivate a distinction between role membership and document relationships. A reviewer role does not grant permission to decide every document. Ownership and reviewer assignment must be checked for read, edit and decision operations. The architecture uses one policy service and negative tests for object-level access.

Explicit workflow logic [S08] informs a transition table and tests for forbidden paths. This project uses a finite-state model and does not claim formal Petri-net verification. Its key invariants are that a decision references a fixed version, only the assigned reviewer can decide, and archived documents cannot be edited.

The resulting contribution is an engineering design and evaluation for a bounded departmental setting. The publications and interview notes do not prove that a new product is commercially superior to established systems. That decision remains conditional on pilot-specific rules, measured evaluation and a build-versus-configure checkpoint.
